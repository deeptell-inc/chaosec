# Popular Summary (PRX Quantum, ≤250 words)

Quantum computers must shield fragile quantum information from constant
disturbance. One unavoidable source of disturbance is measurement itself:
reading out part of a quantum system disturbs the rest. A striking discovery of
recent years is that chaotic quantum dynamics — the kind that scrambles
information across many particles — actually protects encoded information from
this measurement disturbance, behaving like a natural error-correcting code.

This raised a tempting idea. Certain special states called "quantum many-body
scars" resist chaos: they stay simple and coherent where their neighbours become
scrambled. Because they are unusually orderly and long-lived, scars have been
widely imagined as promising carriers of quantum information. Do they, in fact,
protect information better than ordinary chaotic states when measured?

We tested this directly, computing how much quantum information survives when a
logical qubit is stored in scar states versus ordinary "thermal" states while
the system is repeatedly measured. The answer is clear and opposite to the
intuition: chaotic thermal states protect information better, under every
realistic measurement we tried and at every system size. The very order that
defines a scar is what betrays it — a measurement aligned with the scar's hidden
symmetry reads it out easily.

Using an exactly solvable model, we pinpoint the mechanism and show precisely
when — and only when — a finely tuned, unrealistic measurement can rescue scars.
The lesson for measurement-robust quantum memory: scrambling chaos is the asset,
and orderliness is the liability.
